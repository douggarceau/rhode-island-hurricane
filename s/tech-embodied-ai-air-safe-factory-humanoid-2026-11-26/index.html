This Humanoid Has Floppy Arms on Purpose, and Factories Want It

Swiss startup Embodied AI built a wheeled, tendon-driven robot that is safe to bump into and learns a job by watching a human do it.

What happened

A Swiss company called Embodied AI has introduced AIR, a humanoid robot built to work shoulder to shoulder with people on a factory floor, according to Robotics 24/7. The robot was unveiled at Humanoid Europe 2026, the outlet reported, and is now in testing with manufacturers around the world.

The pitch is not a robot that walks, flips or dances for a viral clip. It is a robot that can be bumped into without anyone getting hurt, and that learns a job by watching a person do it first. In a year packed with humanoid launches, that is a deliberately unglamorous angle, and that is the point.

How it works, in plain English

Most robot arms are heavy. Their motors and gearboxes sit right in the joints, so the arm itself carries real weight and real momentum. Embodied AI flipped that layout, Robotics 24/7 reported. AIR keeps its motors, gearboxes and electronics in the torso and moves its arms with tendons, a bit like the cables that run through a bicycle brake or the tendons in a human forearm.

The result is a light arm that carries little mass and gives way when it hits something. The company's line, as the outlet put it, is that safety comes from how the robot is built rather than from software holding it back. That matters because software can fail. Physics does not.

AIR also skips legs. It rolls on wheels, since factory floors are flat and wheels are more stable, according to the report. Legs are the hard, expensive, headline-grabbing part of most humanoids. For a robot meant to stand at a workstation all shift, they are mostly overhead.

The learning side runs on a simple loop. Each new job starts with a human operator controlling the robot, so it is doing useful work from its first shift. Every run gets recorded. The company's AI system learns from those demonstrations until AIR can handle the task on its own, which the company says typically takes about a month. The operator stays nearby to step in on anything the robot has not seen before. And what one robot learns can be passed to the next one, Robotics 24/7 reported.

Who is behind it

Embodied AI was founded by three robotics PhDs. Francesco Stella, the CEO, developed the arm design that grew into AIR during his doctorate at EPFL, the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne, according to the outlet. His co-founders are Kai Junge, the CTO, and Max Polzin, the COO, whose backgrounds run through Cambridge, ETH Zurich and EPFL.

The company is backed by Faber, which led its first funding round, along with TechShop, Look AI Ventures, Plug and Play and Vento, Robotics 24/7 reported. No round size or valuation was disclosed. Embodied AI already sells a separate two-armed industrial cell, and the company says its AI software has been proven in paid factory work on that machine.

The numbers

The figures come from the company and have not been independently checked, so read them that way.

Embodied AI says it went from research project to industrial humanoid in nine months, and that it has signed contracts with major manufacturers worldwide while spending a fraction of what other robot makers have needed. It did not name those customers.

The most specific claim involves cable handling, a fiddly job that still gets done by hand in a lot of factories. According to the company, a leading consumer electronics maker tested five funded startups on the task and none hit its targets. Embodied AI says it reached 98 percent reliability against a 90 percent goal.

On safety paperwork, AIR is designed to ISO/TS 15066, the technical standard covering robots that work in direct contact with people, and the company plans to get CE marking, the European safety certification, in 2027. No price, payload, height or speed figures were released.

What the skeptics will ask

No outside experts were quoted in the launch coverage, and the most impressive numbers belong to unnamed customers. That leaves the obvious questions open. Who are the manufacturers? How does 98 percent hold up over months instead of a test run? And what does it cost compared with a person, or with a plain old industrial arm behind a safety fence?

There is also a crowded field to reckon with. Plenty of better-funded humanoid makers are chasing the same factory jobs, and many of them are building legged, general-purpose bodies. Embodied AI is betting that a narrower, safer, wheeled machine that learns from a coworker gets into real factories faster. Its own claim to have done it on a fraction of rivals' spending is a bet on that approach, not proof of it. Until named customers put these robots on real lines at scale, nobody has won the argument.

Why it matters to you

Stella framed the core issue neatly, per Robotics 24/7: a chatbot that makes something up is an annoyance, but a robot error has physical consequences that cannot be undone. That is the real bottleneck for robots leaving their cages and working next to people.

If designs like this pan out, the first place most people meet a humanoid will not be their kitchen. It will be the factory that assembles their phone, laptop or car parts, where robots take over the repetitive, finicky jobs that change too often for traditional automation. That shapes what things cost, how fast they ship and what factory work looks like for the people still doing it.

What comes next

AIR is in trials with manufacturers now, and the company says it is starting plans for scaled production, according to the outlet. No commercial ship date has been announced. The CE certification target of 2027 is the next hard milestone to watch, along with whether any of those unnamed customers go public.
