RIPTA's Electric Buses: A Small Fleet on the State's Busiest Route

Ocean State Media examined whether RIPTA's battery-electric buses on the R-Line are making a difference, and what stands between the agency and a larger electric fleet.

Rhode Island's public bus system has been adding battery-electric buses since 2022, but they remain a small slice of a fleet of more than 230, according to a Nov. 10 report from Ocean State Media's Possibly podcast, produced with Brown University's Institute for Environment and Society and WBRU. The report found that the electric buses are concentrated on the R-Line, the Rhode Island Public Transit Authority's busiest route, and that plans to bring them to Newport and South County are waiting on federal grant money.

What happened

The episode set out to answer a simple question: do a handful of electric buses make a real difference? Reporters rode along the R-Line, which runs about every 10 minutes and carries more riders than any other RIPTA route, to compare the sound of a diesel bus with an electric one. The report cited a count of 14 electric buses in service, a figure it attributed to 2024, so the current number may differ.

Who said what

Erica Walker, a professor of epidemiology at the Brown University School of Public Health, told the podcast that diesel combustion releases particulate matter and other pollutants that can harm the communities they pass through. She said fine particles can lodge in the lungs and, in some cases, travel to other parts of the body, and that chronic traffic noise can set off the body's stress response, which over time may contribute to heart and mental health problems.

Walker said the R-Line runs through neighborhoods with high asthma rates and heavy traffic pollution, which makes it a sensible place to start. She described the current electric buses as a step in the right direction.

The reporters noted that even though Rhode Island's power grid relies heavily on natural gas, charging an electric bus still produces fewer greenhouse gases than burning diesel, and that the gap would widen as more renewable energy reaches the grid. They also cited studies finding that electric buses tend to cost less to maintain over time, especially when diesel prices are high.

What it means for residents

For riders and neighbors along the R-Line corridor, electric buses mean no tailpipe exhaust at the curb and a much quieter ride, according to the report. The episode noted that cleaner air can reduce public health costs and the school and work days lost to illnesses tied to poor air quality. The trade-off is cost up front: electric buses are more expensive to buy, and each one needs a charging system.

What comes next

RIPTA's next step is expanding electric service to Newport and South County, the report said, but that depends on federal grant funding. Until then, the electric fleet will remain a small share of the buses on Rhode Island roads, concentrated on the route where they reach the most riders.
