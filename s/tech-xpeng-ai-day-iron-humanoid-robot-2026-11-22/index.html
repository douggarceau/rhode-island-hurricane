Xpeng Puts Its Iron Humanoid on Stage Wednesday. The Clock Is Ticking

The Chinese EV maker's AI Day on Nov. 25 is its last big show before its own deadline to mass-produce a walking robot.

What happened

Xpeng, the Guangzhou electric-car company that keeps insisting it is really a robot company, will hold its 2026 AI Day on Wednesday, November 25, at 2 p.m. Beijing time, which is 1 a.m. Eastern. CnEVPost reported that Xpeng announced the date on Weibo on November 19 and gave the event a one-word theme: "Origin."

The headliner is Iron, Xpeng's humanoid robot. According to CnEVPost, Iron is scheduled to come on stage and interact live with the audience. The company also plans to show how its in-house Turing AI chips change the experience inside its cars. No product launches or specs were announced ahead of time.

The timing is pointed. A year ago, at its 2025 AI Day, Xpeng promised Iron would reach mass production by the end of 2026. This is the last big stage before that deadline lands.

How Iron works, in plain English

Iron is a walking computer shaped like a person. TechNode, covering the 2025 reveal, described it as just under 170 centimeters tall, with a skeleton, muscle and skin structure and more than 60 joints. It can stand, sit, lie down and walk.

Its brain is three of Xpeng's Turing chips running a stack of AI models. Electrek reported the setup in three layers: one model to handle conversation, one to read what the robot sees, and one to turn that into movement. Electrek also reported 22 additional degrees of freedom in the hands, which is what lets a robot pick up small, awkward objects rather than just shove boxes.

Power comes from an all-solid-state battery. Xpeng has not published capacity or runtime figures. CnEVPost, citing an October report, said those batteries are now in small-batch production while Iron itself is still in pre-production.

Who is behind it

The pitch belongs to He Xiaopeng, Xpeng's chairman and CEO. His phrase for all of this is "physical AI": AI that lives in bodies, whether that body is a car, a taxi or a humanoid. Ahead of this year's event, CnEVPost reported, He said on Weibo that Xpeng would talk about how AI models and physical bodies evolve together.

He framed three open problems: getting software and hardware to cooperate in the real world, squeezing a big model into a body with limited computing room, and feeding what the body experiences back into the model. That last one is the industry's big bet. Robots that collect their own training data could improve much faster than robots trained only on video.

The numbers

Per CnEVPost, Xpeng's humanoid production line started running in September 2026. On a November 16 earnings call, He said he was confident mass production would start at the end of 2026 or in January 2027, with deliveries beginning in the second quarter of 2027. That is a small slide from the original end-of-2026 target.

The robotaxi side is further along. Xpeng planned three purpose-built robotaxi models for 2026, in five-, six- and seven-seat versions, according to Electrek. Each carries four Turing chips rated at 3,000 TOPS, a measure of AI computing power, and drives on cameras alone, with no lidar and no high-definition maps, TechNode reported. CnEVPost said Xpeng's Yoyo robotaxi was approved in October for citywide Level 4 pilot operations in Guangzhou. Level 4 means no human driver is needed inside a defined area.

Then there are the flying machines. Electrek reported last year that Xpeng's Aridge unit was flight-testing the A868, a six-seat tilt-rotor aircraft with a claimed 500-kilometer range, and building the Land Aircraft Carrier, a six-wheeled vehicle that carries a small eVTOL aircraft. Electrek said that one had more than 7,000 preorders at the time.

What the critics and rivals say

The obvious comparison is Tesla's Optimus. Electrek called Xpeng's robot plan more conservative than the timelines pushed by Tesla and 1X, which have talked up 2026 commercial sales. Xpeng itself was candid last year: according to Electrek, He said a year of trying Iron on screw-tightening work on the assembly line failed on cost and repairs. So the first robots are going into Xpeng's own stores and offices as guides, receptionists and salespeople, not onto factory floors.

He also gave long horizons: three to five years for industrial work and five to ten years for homes, Electrek reported. Electrek was skeptical of the "flying car" label too, calling it a loose term while giving the engineering credit.

And Xpeng will not have the day to itself. CnEVPost noted that rival Nio is holding its own tech day in Guangzhou on the same afternoon, two days before the Guangzhou Auto Show opens on November 27.

Why it matters to you

You are not buying an Iron this year. But the race to make humanoids cheap enough to work in stores, warehouses and eventually homes is being run largely by car companies, because they already know how to build complicated machines by the million. If Xpeng shows a robot that can hold up its end of a live, unscripted exchange, that is a real signal the category is moving from demo reels to products.

The robotaxi piece matters too. A camera-only driving system with no lidar, running in a major city is the same bet Tesla is making. Whichever approach scales first sets the template for how driverless rides arrive in American cities.

What comes next

Watch Wednesday for three things: whether Iron performs live without obvious remote control, whether Xpeng puts a firm date or price on mass production, and whether the robotaxi pilot grows beyond Guangzhou. Then the Guangzhou Auto Show starts on Friday. If last year is a guide, the claims will be big. The proof will be in the Q2 2027 deliveries He has now put on the record.
