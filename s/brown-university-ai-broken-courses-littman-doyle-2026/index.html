"Kind of Broken": Brown's AI Chief on How the Machines Are Rewiring Campus

Brown's provost and its associate provost for AI describe rebuilt courses, anxious math professors and new research teams forming around AI.

Artificial intelligence has "messed up how we used to teach," according to the man Brown University hired to steer it through the AI era. Speaking with Boston Globe columnist Hilary Burns in a conversation published Nov. 17, Michael Littman, Brown's associate provost for artificial intelligence, said that "all our courses at some level are kind of broken now," and that departments across the Providence campus are changing what and how they teach.

Who is behind it

Littman is a longtime Brown computer science professor who has researched AI since the 1980s. Provost Francis J. Doyle III hired him from the National Science Foundation to take the new role, according to the Globe. Doyle traced the idea to an odd coincidence: he received Brown's provost offer on Nov. 30, 2022, the same day OpenAI released ChatGPT. The next summer, he said, he asked President Christina Paxson what the university was doing to help faculty and staff with AI, and she told him to "get to work." The conversation took place at AIxEd's Workforce Summit at Boston University.

What is changing in the classroom

The changes run in two directions at once. Brown now offers AI-focused courses in history, engineering and medicine, and Littman told the Globe they are "cropping up all over the place." Other classes are going the opposite way. "Students can't bring tech in," Doyle said of some courses, which have gone back to hand-marked course packets.

Cheating is part of the picture. The Globe noted that Brown economics professor Roberto Serrano publicly described students cheating on a take-home exam. Littman summed up the shift: "Now they have to make an effort not to cheat."

Policy is still catching up. Doyle said fewer than half of Brown's courses have syllabus policies on when AI is allowed. Littman built a tool that generates a customized AI policy for a course. "It's helping, but there's still anxiety," Doyle said.

Grief in the math department

Some of the most striking comments concerned Brown's mathematicians. Earlier this year Littman suggested the university "might need counselors for the math department." He has collected essays from math professors about AI and told the Globe he can "categorize each essay into one of the five stages of grief."

Research is under pressure too. Littman said faculty can now "generate multiple papers in a weekend" with AI help, and junior faculty are asking whether tenure decisions will come to hinge on raw output.

Why it matters

Brown is one of Rhode Island's biggest engines of research and talent, and how it handles AI will shape what graduates bring into the state's companies, hospitals and labs. The upside is already visible. Doyle described a group spanning applied math, engineering, computer science and the medical school that has come together to work on human RNA sequencing. "I never would have predicted three years ago that those groups could self-assemble," he said.

What comes next

The Globe piece did not lay out a formal timeline. Doyle said Brown "has work to do" on AI syllabus policies, and both leaders described a campus still deciding, course by course, where AI belongs and where it does not. Brown's earlier announcements on AI in teaching and learning are posted on the university's news site.
