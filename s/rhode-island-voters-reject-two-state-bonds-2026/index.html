Voters Approve Three State Bonds, Reject Two for First Time Since 2006

Rhode Islanders backed $445 million for colleges, housing and the green economy but turned down $150 million for economic development and the cultural economy.

Rhode Island voters on Tuesday, Nov. 3, approved three of the five statewide bond questions on the ballot and rejected the other two, the first time the state's voters have turned down a state bond proposal since 2006, NBC 10 WJAR reported based on preliminary, unofficial results.

What passed and what failed

Voters approved a combined $445 million in borrowing and rejected $150 million, according to the station. Each of the three approved questions drew more than 60 percent support in the unofficial count.

The largest approved measure, Question 1, provides $275 million for capital improvements at the state's public colleges and university. Question 2 provides $120 million for housing and homeownership programs, and Question 5 provides $50 million for what the state calls its green economy, which covers environmental and climate-related projects.

Question 3, covering economic development, and Question 4, covering the cultural economy, both failed. Question 4 included funding for a new state history center that had been championed by Secretary of State Gregg Amore, WJAR reported.

What officials said

Amore said he understands the economic pressures facing the state and the competing demands on its budget, but said he was disappointed that the history center did not get over the finish line this year, according to the station.

House Minority Leader Michael Chippendale, a Republican, said many of the no votes likely came from voters who were paying attention to the debt that comes with the bonds, and who understood that they would be responsible for repaying it, WJAR reported.

Voters the station interviewed in Cranston were divided. Some said they supported every question, while others said that some of the projects were worthwhile but that the total amount of borrowing was too much, or questioned where the money to repay it would come from.

The cost of borrowing

Bonds are repaid over many years with interest. According to WJAR, the state has taken on about $2.78 billion in debt service for voter-approved bonds over the past decade, and this year's approved bonds will add about $721 million to that total once interest is included. The station reported that the state repays about $1.60 for every dollar it borrows.

What it means for residents

The approved money will flow over the coming years to building projects at the state's public campuses, to housing programs aimed at the state's tight housing market, and to environmental projects. The rejection of Questions 3 and 4 means the projects tied to those measures, including the proposed history center, will not receive bond money from this ballot.

What comes next

The results remain unofficial until they are certified by the state Board of Elections. Supporters of the rejected projects would need to seek other funding or ask lawmakers to place a new proposal before voters in a future election.
