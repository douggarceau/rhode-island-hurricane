Pawtucket Weighs Leaving Its Leaking 1936 City Hall for Freight Street

With preservation estimated at more than $90 million, Pawtucket is moving city offices to a renovated factory complex, and the incoming mayor says he will review the plan.

Pawtucket's Art Deco City Hall, a 1936 landmark on the National Register of Historic Places, could close as soon as next year as the city shifts municipal offices to a renovated factory complex about a mile away, Ocean State Media reported on Nov. 10. Consultants have estimated that a full historic preservation of the building would cost more than $90 million, and both the outgoing and incoming mayors told the outlet that figure appears out of reach.

What happened

According to the report, the city has been moving some functions to what it calls a City Hall annex at 100 Freight Street, a partly renovated factory complex. Bid documents prepared by the administration of Mayor Donald Grebien already refer to the property as the "New City Hall." The council chamber at the Freight Street site is already in use and is set up for livestreaming and video calls, Ocean State Media reported.

The original building, built during the New Deal with help from Works Progress Administration funds, has struggled for years with roof leaks. The outlet reported that water damage has closed the entire fourth floor, that flaking plaster and paint mark interior walls, and that the city runs plug-in air purifiers throughout the building to meet health and safety standards. Its unfinished tower, which city officials regard as a major source of leaks, has repeatedly dropped bits of brick and concrete onto the sidewalk, and scaffolding now shields both entrances.

The budget for a larger second phase of renovation at Freight Street is not public while the city reviews competitive construction bids, a spokesperson for Grebien confirmed to Ocean State Media. Voters have approved bonds for patch repairs to the old building several times over the years, but some of those fixes lasted only a few years, the outlet reported.

Who said what

Grebien, who has served 16 years as mayor, the longest tenure in the city's history, and who lost his bid for re-election this fall, told the outlet that the building's operational value matters more than its historic value, and that the cost of full preservation seemed beyond the city's reach.

Mayor-elect Adam Greenman said he plans to review the relocation closely. He told Ocean State Media he is concerned about moving City Hall out of downtown to a site without direct public transit, and he called the decision one of the most complicated he faces. Greenman also said the money needed for preservation could address many other city needs, and he suggested that less costly options, such as renting another building downtown, may exist. Both men said the original building may have a better chance of survival in the hands of a private developer.

A local historian interviewed by the outlet praised the building's craftsmanship and noted that Rhode Island's preservation movement of the 1960s and 1970s helped save it once before, while voicing concern that public interest in preservation has faded as residents focus on roads and bridges.

What it means for residents

For residents, the shift could change where they go to pay bills, attend council meetings and get permits. Ocean State Media reported that the Freight Street site offers more parking and better cell reception, but it sits away from the downtown core, where new apartments have opened near the commuter rail station and more are planned near the soccer stadium.

What comes next

The city will decide on the second phase of Freight Street work after it finishes evaluating construction bids, and Greenman will review the plans once he takes office. The report described the move as possibly temporary. No City Council vote on the future of the 1936 building has been reported, and its long-term fate may depend on whether a private buyer comes forward with another use for it.
