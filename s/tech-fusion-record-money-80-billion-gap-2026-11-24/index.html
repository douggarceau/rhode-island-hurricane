Fusion Has Record Money. It Still Needs $80 Billion More.

A new global outlook counts more fusion machines and more private cash than ever, and a long, expensive road before any of it lights your kitchen.

What happened

The International Atomic Energy Agency has published its fourth World Fusion Outlook, a 128-page check-up on the effort to build power plants that run on the same reaction as the sun. According to Nature World News, the report was released Nov. 12 in Vienna at the third meeting of the agency's World Fusion Energy Group.

The headline numbers point two ways at once. Private fusion companies pulled in a record $4.5 billion of new funding in the year to July 2026, the outlook reports. At the same time, a London analysis firm that worked on the report estimates the industry may need roughly $80 billion more over the next two decades before the first commercial plants come online.

How it works, in plain English

Today's nuclear plants split heavy atoms apart. Fusion does the opposite: it squeezes light atoms, usually forms of hydrogen, so hard and so hot that they merge, releasing energy. The fuel is abundant and the process does not produce the long-lived waste that fission does. The catch is that the fuel has to be held at temperatures far hotter than the core of the sun, and kept stable long enough to give back more energy than the machine burns.

Most designs use powerful magnets to trap a superheated gas called plasma in a ring-shaped chamber, known as a tokamak. Others use lasers or pulses of electricity to crush tiny fuel pellets. Nobody has yet turned any of these into a machine that sends steady power to the grid.

Who is behind it

The outlook counts 174 fusion facilities worldwide that are operating, under construction or planned, up from 134 in 2022, according to the American Nuclear Society's review as reported by Nature World News. Of 111 public facilities, 85 are running. Of 63 private ones, 13 are. The report says the number of private facilities has tripled since 2021.

Asia and the Pacific lead with 78 facilities, followed by North America with 53 and Europe with 38. The giant international project ITER, under construction in France, is progressing ahead of its 2024 baseline schedule, the report finds.

Google DeepMind wrote a chapter on artificial intelligence. It argues AI can shrink simulations that now take weeks or months, help control machines in real time and speed up the search for new materials. It also notes a practical problem: much fusion research data is scattered and hard to use for training. The IAEA is pushing a shared Fusion Data Lake to fix that.

The numbers

Twenty-six fusion power plants are in planning, the outlook reports. Eight aim for the early 2030s, nine for the late 2030s, two for the 2040s and one for the 2050s. Six give no date at all.

The money estimates vary depending on who is counting. The Fusion Industry Association says private firms have raised more than $14 billion since 2021. FusionX, the London firm that analyzed 92 private developers for the report, puts committed private capital at about $21.9 billion, with more than 90 percent of it raised in the past five years. FusionX projects a record $7.6 billion in private funding in 2026 if the current pace holds, and expects some developers to merge.

Private fusion firms spent more than $530 million with suppliers in 2025, according to the report, and gaps are showing up in manufacturing capacity.

What the skeptics say

Nature World News flags that the biggest funding figures come from industry-linked sources, and that the Fusion Industry Association's chief executive helped draft and review the report. The outlet also points out that no AI system has yet solved the hard problems: sustained net energy, materials that survive years of punishment, and producing fuel at power-plant scale.

Its other warning is worth keeping in mind every time a lab announces a record. A hotter plasma or a longer burn is a lab result, not electricity on the grid. A EUROfusion study adapted for the outlook models fusion supplying anywhere from a few percent to 15 to 20 percent of world electricity by 2100, and finds that how fast plants scale up matters more than the date of the first one.

Why it matters to you

Short version: not on this year's power bill. Nature World News reports that nothing in the outlook points to fusion lowering electricity costs this decade.

The longer version is about the 2030s and beyond. AI data centers, electric cars and heat pumps are all pushing demand for electricity up. A working fusion plant would be a source of steady power without carbon emissions that does not depend on the weather. That is why investors keep writing checks despite the timeline, and why the gap between record funding and the $80 billion estimate is the number to watch.

What comes next

The work is moving from physics papers to permits and purchase orders. On Nov. 20, the American Nuclear Society reported that New Mexico regulators licensed Pacific Fusion to handle radioactive materials for deuterium-tritium experiments at a facility under construction in Albuquerque. A week earlier, the society reported that the U.K.'s STEP prototype program had begun major procurements.

Nature World News says the milestones to track are ITER's construction progress, first operations at private demonstration machines in the U.S. and Europe, and the IAEA's next annual outlook.
