Waymo Takes the Exit: Robotaxis Pulled Off Highways in Five Metros

After California called its driverless highway runs a public safety hazard, Waymo stopped highway rides everywhere it offered them, with no restart date.

What happened

Waymo has pulled its driverless cars off the highway. In every market where its robotaxis had been carrying paying riders at highway speed, the cars now stick to surface streets. According to a CNN report republished by Scripps News on November 17, the pause covers all five areas where Waymo had allowed highway driving: the San Francisco Bay Area, Los Angeles County, Phoenix, Miami and Austin. Waymo operates in 13 U.S. areas in total, CNN reported.

The trigger was California. The state's Department of Motor Vehicles issued a restriction on November 11 that bars Waymo from running or testing driverless cars on California highways, and made it public on November 17, according to a report from The National Desk with AP contributions, carried by ABC News 4. The DMV described the driverless highway runs as an imminent hazard to public safety. Outside California, the pause is Waymo's own call, the same report said, and the company has given no end date.

What the cars got wrong

The DMV pointed to two incidents this month, per The National Desk. On November 5, three Waymo vehicles drove into an active construction closure on U.S. 101 near Burlingame. On November 6, a Waymo tried to pass California Highway Patrol cars that were running a traffic break on I-280 in San Francisco. A traffic break is when officers weave across lanes to slow everyone down, usually to clear something ahead. Passing it is the one thing you are not supposed to do.

The regulator also cited similar incidents from May 18, which happened despite an earlier restriction and fixes Waymo had already promised, according to the same report. CNN described the core complaint as repeated incursions into active work zones, putting road crews, other drivers and equipment at risk.

How it works, in plain English

A robotaxi reads the road with cameras, lidar and radar, then software decides what to do next. City streets are slow and crowded, but they are also full of the kind of situations the software has seen millions of times. Highways flip that. Speeds are high, mistakes are costly, and the strange stuff is rarer: a lane that suddenly ends in cones, a crew working behind a single flashing truck, a patrol car deliberately slowing traffic. These are edge cases, and edge cases are where self-driving systems still stumble.

Construction is a particular headache because the map the car carries no longer matches the road. The car has to trust what it sees over what it expects, and it has to read human signals, like a flagger or a line of cones, the way a human driver would. The November incidents suggest that part is not solved.

This has happened before

This is the second time this year. TechCrunch reported on May 21 that Waymo had already suspended highway rides in San Francisco, Los Angeles, Phoenix and Miami to improve how its cars handle construction zones. At the time, Waymo told TechCrunch it was folding recent lessons into its software and expected to bring highway routes back soon. They did come back. Six months later, they are gone again.

Waymo first offered highway rides in late 2025, according to TechCrunch. The pitch was simple: highways connect riders to airports and cut trip times. TechCrunch noted that on the Bay Area peninsula, highway routing cut rides that used to take 45 minutes to over an hour.

The numbers

Waymo's own safety page claims its vehicles are involved in 95 percent fewer crashes resulting in serious injury or worse than human drivers, CNN reported. The company also told reporters the U.S. 101 incident caused no injuries or collisions, per The National Desk.

The other number that matters is growth. Waymo had set a goal of as many as one million paid rides per week by the end of 2026, TechCrunch reported in May. Losing highways in five markets does not stop the cars, but it does make some trips slower.

What the critics say

The DMV is the loudest critic right now, and it is the one with the power to say no. Before this, a CNN analysis in June found hundreds of incidents in which robotaxis allegedly made dangerous moves, such as running red lights, heading into oncoming traffic, ignoring emergency closures and coming within inches of pedestrians. Waymo responded at the time that it was working on improvements, that the incidents were largely isolated, and that most rides go smoothly, CNN reported.

Why it matters to you

If you ride Waymo in one of these five metros, expect longer trips for now, since the car will take surface streets where it used to hop on the highway. If you do not ride Waymo yet, this is still the story to watch. Waymo is one of the biggest names in driverless rides in the U.S., so where it hits a wall says a lot about where the technology stands. Right now that wall is a row of orange cones.

What comes next

Waymo is still testing on highways with trained specialists behind the wheel, and its city service keeps running, according to The National Desk. A Waymo spokesperson told CNN the company is working with the California DMV and other state officials to get highway rides back. The DMV says the restriction stays until Waymo shows corrective fixes that work. Nobody has put a date on that.
