Minority- and Women-Owned Firms Win Record Share of R.I. State Contracts

Businesses owned by minorities and women received 18.1 percent of state purchasing in fiscal 2026, the highest mark in the program's 40-year history.

Minority- and women-owned businesses received 18.1 percent of Rhode Island's state purchases and contracts in fiscal year 2026, the highest share since the state's minority business enterprise program began 40 years ago, according to a new state report described by The Boston Globe this week. The result also clears the 15 percent target set in state law in 2023, a level the state had not reached in the prior decade.

What the report found

According to the Globe's account of the report, about $124 million of roughly $688 million in state contract value went to certified minority- and women-owned firms. Minority business enterprises accounted for 8.44 percent of the total, and women business enterprises for 9.65 percent.

The jump was a sizable one. The combined share was 13.4 percent in fiscal 2025, the Globe reported, and the program's low point came in 2015, when the figure stood at 4.07 percent.

By the Globe's breakdown, firms owned by white women received the largest portion of the $124 million, about $62.8 million. Firms owned by Portuguese men received about $27.2 million, Hispanic men about $17.3 million, Black men about $9.9 million and Asian men about $3.1 million.

How the goals have changed

A state requirement adopted in 1986 called for at least 10 percent of state contract and purchasing dollars to go to minority- and women-owned businesses, a goal the state often missed, according to the Globe. A 2023 law raised the target to 15 percent and requires prime vendors, including large contractors that hire subcontractors, to show good-faith efforts to meet it.

What officials said

Pastor Chris Abhulime, a deputy chief of staff to Gov. Daniel J. McKee, told the Globe the administration was proud of the results after six years of work. He described the program as a tool for economic development among the state's many very small businesses and as part of the governor's goal of raising incomes.

Keith W. Stokes, associate director of the state Division of Equity, Diversity and Inclusion, told the paper that more than 90 percent of Rhode Island's small businesses have 10 or fewer employees, and that many have the capacity to do state work but lack the resources to bid. He described the program as a matter of helping small disadvantaged businesses compete, which he said the state is legally obligated to do.

Stokes credited several efforts for the increase, according to the Globe: a pilot called Rhode Map to Success that offers procurement training, pre-bid help and networking with buyers; a new Rhode Island Commerce grant program providing technical assistance to small disadvantaged businesses; a dedicated contract compliance officer funded in the state budget about three years ago; and training for state agencies and prime vendors on the newer requirements.

State Sen. Tiara T. Mack, a Providence Democrat who co-chairs the legislature's Black, Latino, Indigenous, Asian-American and Pacific Islander Caucus, called the increase encouraging but said more progress is needed. She told the Globe she wants to look more closely at how many Black and Latino owners received contracts, and noted that some states do not include Portuguese-owned firms in similar programs. The Globe also noted that the state reached the goal while the Trump administration has moved to scale back federal diversity programs.

What it means for residents

For small business owners, the report signals that state purchasing, which covers everything from construction to supplies and services, is reaching more certified firms. Businesses that want to compete for state work must first be certified through the state's Minority Business Enterprise Compliance Office, and state officials told the Globe they plan more training sessions to help owners get certified.

What comes next

The state's budget process for the next fiscal year will determine funding for the compliance staff and training programs that officials credited for the gains. Lawmakers including Mack have said they intend to propose changes to the program in the coming legislative session.
