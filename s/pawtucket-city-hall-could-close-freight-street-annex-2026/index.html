Pawtucket's 1936 City Hall Could Close Next Year as Offices Shift to Annex

Leaks and falling masonry threaten the Art Deco landmark, and the incoming mayor wants a close look at the move to Freight Street.

Pawtucket's Art Deco City Hall, a nationally recognized landmark that has anchored the city's downtown since 1936, could close as soon as next year because of chronic roof leaks and crumbling masonry, according to reporting by Ocean State Media. Outgoing Mayor Donald Grebien is already moving some city functions to a renovated factory complex about a mile away, while the incoming mayor says he wants to take a close look at the plan before the city commits to it.

What happened

The building's empty tower is considered a major source of persistent leaks that have left interior walls with flaking plaster and paint, Ocean State Media reported. Water damage has forced the city to close the entire fourth floor, and the city runs plug-in air purifiers throughout the building to meet health and safety standards. Bricks and concrete from the tower have fallen to the sidewalk many times, and scaffolding that shields both entrances has become effectively permanent.

The problems are not new. Archived Pawtucket Times articles show city officials discussed removing the tower as early as 1960, and voters have repeatedly approved bonds for repairs, some of which lasted only a few years, according to the report.

Consultants estimated that a full historic preservation of City Hall would cost more than $90 million. The city bought the property at 100 Freight Street for $14.8 million and has spent another $8 million renovating part of it, Ocean State Media reported. City bid documents refer to the site as the "New City Hall." A city spokesperson said the budget for a larger second phase of renovation is not public while the city reviews competitive construction bids.

Who said what

Grebien, who is finishing a 16-year tenure after losing his re-election bid this fall, told Ocean State Media he rejects the approach of patching the building piece by piece and said the cost of full preservation appears out of reach. He said the building's value to the city now is more operational than historic.

Incoming Mayor Adam Greenman, who already works out of an unfinished office at the annex, said he will review the plans closely. He told the outlet he has misgivings about moving City Hall from downtown to a location without direct public transit, and he said that $92 million could pay for many other needs. He said cheaper options, such as renting another building, may be available. Both men said they believe the original building has a better chance of survival in the hands of a private developer.

Robert Geake, a Rhode Island historian who lives in Pawtucket, praised the building's craftsmanship and said the state's preservation movement of the 1960s and 1970s saved it from an earlier demolition threat. He told Ocean State Media he worries public appetite for preservation has faded.

What it means for residents

The annex offers better cell reception and more parking, and its City Council chamber is already in use and equipped for livestreaming and video calls, according to the report. For residents who walk or take the bus downtown, however, a move would put City Hall farther from the city's center. Ocean State Media noted that downtown Pawtucket is changing quickly, with new apartments near the recently opened commuter rail station and more planned near the soccer stadium, even as the neighborhood is set to lose both a large public high school and its City Hall in quick succession.

What comes next

Listing on the National Register of Historic Places does not guarantee the building against demolition, the report noted. No final decision has been made on the building's future, and Greenman's review of the move will shape the next steps once he takes office. Whether a private buyer steps forward with a new use may ultimately decide whether the 1936 landmark survives.
