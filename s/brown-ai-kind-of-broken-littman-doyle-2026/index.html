'Kind of Broken Now': Brown's AI Chiefs on How Machines Are Rewiring Campus

Brown's provost and its associate provost for AI told a Boston audience that AI is upending courses, cheating, math and research on College Hill.

Brown University's top AI official has a blunt verdict on the state of the college classroom. "All our courses at some level are kind of broken now," Michael Littman, Brown's associate provost for artificial intelligence, said at a recent fireside chat, according to a Nov. 17 Boston Globe College Town column by Hilary Burns.

What happened

Burns moderated the conversation with Littman and Brown Provost Francis J. Doyle III at AIxEd's Workforce Summit at Boston University, which the Globe says took place the previous week. The column, headlined "Cheating students, grieving mathematicians, new research collaborations: How Brown is being roiled by AI," lays out how the Providence campus is adjusting in real time.

Who is behind it

The Globe reports that Doyle got Brown's provost offer on Nov. 30, 2022, the same day OpenAI released ChatGPT. He later hired Littman from the National Science Foundation. Littman, a longtime Brown computer science professor, has researched AI since the 1980s, and the column describes his job title as one that is "proliferating on campuses across the nation."

The details

Cheating is the obvious flashpoint. Brown made news earlier this year when an economics professor described students cheating on a take-home exam, the Globe notes. Littman's take on today's students: "Now they have to make an effort not to cheat."

Some faculty are going analog. Doyle said some courses "require students to check their devices at the door," adding, "Students can't bring tech in." At the same time, Littman said Brown now offers AI-focused courses in history, engineering and medicine.

Rules are still catching up. According to the column, Doyle said fewer than half of Brown's courses have syllabus policies spelling out when AI is allowed. Littman built a tool that helps faculty automate a customized course policy. "It's helping, but there's still anxiety," Doyle said.

The math department is feeling it most. The Globe reports that Littman suggested earlier this year that Brown "might need counselors for the math department," and he called mathematicians "the canary in the coal mine."

Why it matters

The flip side is a research boom. Doyle described an RNA sequencing effort that pulls together faculty from applied math, engineering, computer science and the medical school. "I never would have predicted three years ago that those groups could self-assemble," he said, per the Globe.

It is also changing the pace of academic life. Littman said faculty can now "generate multiple papers in a weekend," and the column reports that junior faculty are worried about what that means for tenure expectations.

For Rhode Island, how it handles AI in the lecture hall and the lab will shape the graduates and startups that come out of College Hill.

What comes next

Brown's own newsroom shows the AI work spreading across disciplines this month. On Nov. 2 the university announced that a Brown archaeologist will co-lead an AI project, supported by Schmidt Sciences, to advance understanding of ancient Andean empires. On Nov. 9 it published research urging an end to the "whack-a-mole" fight against AI deepfakes by addressing the wider "ecosystem." The full College Town column is on the Boston Globe's site, and Brown posts its AI research news at brown.edu/news.
