R.I. Minority Business Contracting Hits Record 18.1% in Fiscal 2026

Minority- and women-owned firms received about $124 million in state contracts, topping the 15 percent goal for the first time.

Businesses owned by minorities and women received 18.1 percent of Rhode Island's state purchasing and contract dollars in fiscal year 2026, the highest share in the 40-year history of the state's minority business enterprise program, The Boston Globe reported this week. The figure tops the 15 percent target set in state law in 2023, a level the state had not reached in the previous decade, according to the report.

What happened

According to the Globe, minority- and women-owned firms received about $124 million of the roughly $688 million in state contract value counted in the 2026 fiscal year. Minority business enterprises accounted for 8.44 percent of that spending and women business enterprises for 9.65 percent, the newspaper reported.

The program is run by the Rhode Island Division of Equity, Diversity and Inclusion, and Gov. Daniel J. McKee's office has promoted it, the Globe said. Under a requirement dating to 1986, at least 10 percent of state contract and purchase dollars were supposed to go to these businesses, but the state often fell short of that mark, according to the report. A 2023 law raised the goal to 15 percent.

The Globe also broke down the roughly $124 million by ownership group. Firms owned by white women received $62.8 million, firms owned by Portuguese men $27.2 million, firms owned by Hispanic men $17.3 million, firms owned by Black men $9.9 million and firms owned by Asian men $3.1 million, according to the report.

How the state got here

The Globe described several state efforts behind the increase. A pilot called Rhode Map to Success offers procurement training, technical assistance, help before bids are submitted and business networking. Rhode Island Commerce started a grant program that pays for basic technical assistance for small disadvantaged businesses. Three years ago, the state budget paid for a contract compliance officer position, the newspaper said.

The 2023 law also requires prime vendors on state contracts to show good-faith efforts to include certified firms, and the state now trains both agencies and vendors on those rules, according to the report.

Who said what

Pastor Chris Abhulime, a deputy chief of staff to McKee, called the result exciting, the Globe reported.

Keith W. Stokes, associate director of the Division of Equity, Diversity and Inclusion, told the newspaper the program addresses small disadvantaged businesses rather than being a diversity initiative. He described it as "economic development," according to the report.

State Sen. Tiara T. Mack, a Providence Democrat, called the increase encouraging but said the state still has a ways to go, the Globe reported. Mack said the figure is probably still not enough given changes in federal policy under the Trump administration, and said she wants a closer look at how many Black and Latino owners received state contracts.

What it means for residents

State contracts cover a wide range of goods and services bought with taxpayer money. Supporters of the program say a larger share for small and disadvantaged firms spreads that spending across more Rhode Island businesses. The figures show, however, that the share varies widely among ownership groups, with firms owned by white women receiving about half of the total, according to the Globe's breakdown.

What comes next

Stokes's office is training small businesses and startups on how to become certified, including sessions at the Center for Southeast Asians in Providence, the Globe reported. The report did not give timelines for further changes. Businesses interested in certification can find program information on the Division of Equity, Diversity and Inclusion website.

The results arrive as the state prepares for a change in leadership. Gov.-elect Helena Foulkes, who won the Nov. 3 election, is expected to take office in early January, according to WPRI 12.
